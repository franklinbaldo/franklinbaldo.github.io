The first song I ever recorded on [Suno](https://suno.com/@franklinbaldo) was a Borges in _viola_. It came out crooked, the voice tripping on a syllable he probably never said aloud. I played it three times. Then I went to bed.

There are 92 today. None of them came from a plan — they came from stubbornness. I wanted to hear things nobody was going to record for me, so I started recording them with a model.

What I enjoy most is inventing voices. I keep a small recurring cast: **Irineu**, ceremonial and slow, somewhere between liturgy and a porch story; **Caetano**, my excuse to attempt a Brazilian voice drenched in tropicália without offending the original; **Maria Bonita**, who shows up when I want leather, backlands, and contained violence; and **two Borges** — one in Portuguese, one in English — because I have never decided whether I prefer the translated Borges or the Borges who recites as if he were decoding a cipher. None of these are anyone's real voice. They are masks. And like every good mask, they say more about who wears them than about who is being imitated.

The songs live in collections that, reading them back, feel like chapters of a book I would not know how to write in prose: **Meditação**, 11 slow-ambient tracks; **Aleph caipira**, 8, Borges meeting the _viola_, the point that contains every point seen from a porch in Rondônia; **Brazil philosophy**, 10, attempts to set to music the ideas I keep chewing on; **Borges (PT)**, 11, and **Borges (EN)**, 3, because translating Borges into song is a habit I have no intention of dropping.

People click expecting a single and find an essay. Fair enough. That is how I listen too.

A slightly sideways observation: describing sound in text is a cousin to describing color for someone who sees in grey. Suno's prompt forced me to find adjectives for things I had always heard without naming. I think I learned more about what I listen for from writing prompts than from listening to music. This might be wrong — it could be a novelty effect — but it is the suspicion that follows me every time I open the interface.

Suno is the kind of tool that forces you to explain what you want to hear before you hear it. You discover, mid-prompt, that you didn't want _bossa_ — you wanted _bossa with short breath and a dirty guitar_. The song is already there, before the audio.

I don't know if this is music. I know it is writing.

17 followers. No algorithm pushing, no metric to chase. Just the folder growing. The dog wakes when the ambient stops, never when it starts.
