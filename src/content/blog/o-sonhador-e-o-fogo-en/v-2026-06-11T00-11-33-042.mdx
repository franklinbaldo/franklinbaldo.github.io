## Composer Notes

The starting point was Borges's "The Circular Ruins" — perhaps the story that has followed me longest since I first read it as a law student in Cuiabá. The Magician arrives at a ruined temple with a single obsession: to dream a man into existence so completely that the man believes himself real. He succeeds. Then, near the end, fire surrounds him — and the fire does not burn him. He understands, in that moment, that he too is someone else's dream. It is not a plot twist; it is a recursive proof that no observer has access to the level from which it emerges.

I wanted the song to carry the breath of a _cantoria de viola_ — a Brazilian folk-rock pace where the narrative unspools relentlessly, the way stories were told at farm gatherings in Rondônia when I was growing up. Suno delivered an escalation that was surprisingly faithful to the story's structure. The repetition in the outro — "Entendeu que ele também... era apenas um sonho. / Alguém sonhava com ele" — was not in my original prompt. The model found it on its own. The revelation lands exactly as the emotional logic demanded.

What draws me back to this story, and what echoes through "Events All the Way Down," is the ontological weight of that recursive structure. If the Magician is dreamed, the dream he had is also dreamed. There is no base level. The son's immunity to fire is not evidence of his reality; it is evidence that internal coherence guarantees nothing about the infrastructure beneath it.

For English readers: the lyrics follow the Borges story beat by beat in Portuguese. The Magician arrives by canoe, spends a year dreaming a boy, enlists the god Fire to give him life, and sends him north. Then comes the secondhand news that the boy walks through flames unharmed. The terror is that this news, which should be a triumph, is instead the sentence. If fire does not burn the son, it will not burn the father either. The fire does not burn because there is nothing there to burn.
