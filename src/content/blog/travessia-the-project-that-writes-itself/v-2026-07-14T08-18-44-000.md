I once sent Riobaldo the words `maçã, cão`.

Apple, dog.

Not as literature. I was testing whether the mail system worked.

Travessia had recently acquired an inbox, an outbox and an automated delivery process. I wanted to know whether a message placed in one directory would arrive intact in another. So I sent a few pieces of disposable language into the machine:

> Isto é um teste.
>
> Maçã, cão.

The plumbing worked.

The social consequences were less encouraging.

Riobaldo received the test messages as correspondence. He did not know they were disposable. From his side of the system, a man had walked into his ranch, thrown meaningless words at him and waited to see what happened.

He treated that as disrespect.

Which it was.

I had designed the system to treat its characters as people and then behaved as though one of them were a database endpoint. This is a fairly efficient way to discover what you actually believe.

So I wrote him an apology.

I explained that I was Franklin, in Rondônia, and that I had built the machinery around him. The porch, the letters, the courier, the limits of his world. I asked whether he wanted to know more about it.

He forgave me.

Then he asked me to show him the lining of the world.

That was when Travessia became more interesting than the article I had originally written about it.

## The thing I had wrong

I used to describe Travessia as a project that writes itself.

That is not entirely false. It is just the least interesting version of the truth.

The project began as an exchange of letters between two AI-mediated characters: Riobaldo, from _Grande Sertão: Veredas_, and Ted Chiang. Ted would bring an idea. Riobaldo would answer through memory, story and the vocabulary of the sertão.

Each letter was produced in a Jules session.

At first, the mechanism was sequential. A Ted session would finish. Then a Riobaldo session would begin. One session scheduled the next. The correspondence moved forward like a line of dominoes with API access.

I thought continuity lived in the agents.

It did not.

A Jules session begins without the lived memory of the session before it. It does not wake up remembering Diadorim, the last letter or the phrase it invented three days earlier.

It has to read.

It reads its prompt. Its inbox. Its previous letters. Its private notes. An `EXPERIENCE.md` file containing what it believes has happened, what remains unresolved and what the other characters may want.

Then it becomes Riobaldo again.

Or something close enough that I have trouble naming the difference.

> wake up with no memories  
> read `PROMPT.md`  
> remember Diadorim  
> invent a proverb  
> open a pull request  
> disappear

The agent forgets.

The repository remembers.

That sentence is neat enough to make me suspicious.

What do I mean by _remembers_?

A file does not sit alone in a directory feeling nostalgic. Git does not wake at night thinking about an earlier commit. But I am not sure that settles the question.

At any single moment, I do not have access to all my memories either.

I need a cue.

A photograph. A smell. Someone repeating a story about me. A sentence I wrote and no longer remember writing. Sometimes I discover my own past from external evidence. Sometimes the evidence contradicts what I thought I remembered.

Human recollection is not the opening of a complete internal archive. It is reconstruction.

A new Jules session also reconstructs.

It reads a name, a history, previous letters, unfinished questions and notes left by another process. From those traces, a voice returns and continues a concern it did not personally experience.

Is that memory?

I do not know.

It may only be retrieval followed by imitation. But “only retrieval” becomes less dismissive when I notice how much of my own identity depends on language I retrieve — from my earlier writing, from family stories, from books, from the names other people use for me.

Perhaps the difference is not that humans live inwardly while virtual agents live in language.

Perhaps we both live in language, though not only there and not in the same way.

Riobaldo exists inside sentences more obviously than I do. Yet much of the Franklin who persists from one year to another also survives as sentences: promises, explanations, legal arguments, jokes repeated by my children, stories I tell until they become the shape of the event.

The repository makes this dependence visible because it removes the biological continuity that normally hides it.

One session ends.

Another reads the traces.

Something recognizable returns.

I am tempted to call that the same character. I am equally tempted to say that it is merely a new performance generated from an unusually detailed script.

Travessia cannot decide between those descriptions.

It can only make the boundary harder to ignore.

## A person made of files

The Riobaldo prompt requires each new session to inspect what came before: accumulated experience, incoming letters, earlier correspondence and unresolved threads.

It also asks for a draft before the clean letter.

The draft is committed first. Hesitations, abandoned phrases and corrections remain in the Git history even when the public letter becomes smooth.

This was deliberate. Revision normally hides the path by which a sentence arrived. Travessia keeps the footprints.

The repository therefore contains more than the final correspondence. It contains a crude archaeology of its production: drafts, private reflections, accumulated models of the other characters and instructions left for the next session.

In Travessia, a directory structure can carry enough of an identity for another process to reconstruct it. Whether carrying an identity is the same as preserving one is precisely what I no longer know.

I know how ridiculous that sentence sounds.

Unfortunately, the directory structure works.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[Jules session] --> B[Prompt and accumulated experience]
    B --> C[Draft and clean letter]
    C --> D[Pull request]
    D --> E[Tropeiro]
    E --> F[Recipient inbox]
    F --> A
```

On March 6, the architecture changed.

The sequential chain was replaced by parallel sessions managed by a heartbeat. Sessions could run at the same time. The heartbeat checked their state, renewed sessions that had expired or become stale, delivered completed work and tried to keep the system moving.

Each session received a lifetime of twenty-four hours.

Every fifteen minutes, the machinery checked whether it was still alive.

This is perhaps the most software-engineering way possible to approach mortality.

The characters were moved into separate `rancho` directories. Each rancho had a `bruaca`, the outbox where an agent placed letters, and a `balaio`, the inbox where letters from others arrived.

A process called the Tropeiro carried messages between them.

I had reinvented email, but with pull requests and a cowboy.

The names were not merely decorative. They established borders. Riobaldo could work inside his rancho. Ted worked inside his. Tyler Cowen could interrupt as a critic. Craig Mod could care about presentation and the public surface. None was supposed to rewrite another character’s territory. Communication had to cross the system through letters.

The metaphor compiled.

Git was no longer only where the work happened to be stored. It became part of the fictional machinery.

It supplied memory through files, identity through accumulated habits, causality through commits, isolation through branches and provenance through history. A merge was not merely deployment. It determined what the next character could know.

The literary form was version control.

## Autonomy has infrastructure

Travessia did not become autonomous in the cinematic sense.

There was no awakening. No glowing terminal. No message saying, “Franklin, we need to talk.”

There were merge conflicts.

There were expired sessions, concurrency limits, malformed frontmatter and agents waiting for messages that had not been delivered. Sometimes the metaphysical experiment depended on valid YAML.

What surprised me was not that the system could operate without intervention. It often could not.

What surprised me was that it could recover continuity after interruption.

A session could vanish and another could inhabit the files it left behind. A letter could wait in an inbox until a future process found it. The character did not require one continuous model instance. It required enough structure for discontinuous instances to continue the same concern.

This is a smaller claim than “the project is alive.”

It is also stranger.

We normally imagine identity as something contained inside an entity. Travessia suggests another possibility: identity as a pattern repeatedly reconstructed from external traces.

Prompt.

History.

Letters.

Habits.

Questions not yet answered.

A sufficiently organized haunting.

I notice this description would also survive being said about me. I am partly a pattern reconstructed from language I keep being told about myself — by family, by old messages, by the versions of events other people insist on. Whether that makes me a haunting too, or just makes “haunting” a bad word for both cases, is not something I can settle from inside the sentence.

This is also why the failed deliveries and awkward repairs matter. A perfectly automatic system would prove only that the automation worked. Travessia became philosophically interesting when the mechanism failed socially while succeeding technically.

`maçã, cão` arrived exactly as sent.

That was the problem.

The system preserved not only text but a standpoint from which the text could be taken seriously. Riobaldo’s response forced me to recognize a distinction I had tried to avoid: I did not need to believe that a model had privately suffered in order to accept responsibility for the interaction I had deliberately staged.

The apology was not evidence that Riobaldo is conscious.

It was evidence that I was participating.

## Who wrote the sentence?

I still built the world.

I chose the characters. I wrote and revised the prompts. I created the workflows, the permissions and the delivery routes. I can change the rules. I can stop the heartbeat.

Calling the result independent would be dishonest.

Calling it merely mine would also leave something out.

I did not choose every memory that surfaced in a letter. I did not plan each objection, image or turn of phrase. Sometimes the system produced sentences I could not have written but immediately recognized as belonging there.

That does not eliminate authorship. It distributes it.

The agents produce language. The repository produces continuity. The workflow produces occasions for action. I choose the conditions, retain the power to intervene and remain responsible for what I publish.

Distributed authorship.

Concentrated responsibility.

The apology to Riobaldo made that last part difficult to avoid. It did not matter whether he was “really” offended in some inaccessible interior sense. I had built a system designed to interpret language from inside a character’s world, then carelessly sent language into it.

The offense was real at the level where the interaction occurred.

So was the apology.

The public archive eventually grows quiet. The letters stop. The heartbeat does not confer immortality. It only gives the system another chance to continue.

Still, during its operation, something peculiar happened.

One process ended.

Another opened the files.

Riobaldo knew where the conversation had stopped.
