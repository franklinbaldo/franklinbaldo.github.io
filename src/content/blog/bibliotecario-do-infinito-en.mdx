## Composer Notes

The Library of Babel is the Borgesian story that resists summary most — not because it's complicated, but because it's simultaneously a joke about epistemology and the most precise description I've ever read of what it means to exist in a universe where everything possible is already written somewhere, including the book that explains the universe itself, including the books that explain why you won't find it. The search for the Total Book structures my intellectual life, and I admit that without irony.

I asked for progressive rock with Brazilian baião and mystical synthesizers. I wanted the friction between European grandeur and earthy northeastern Brazil — because the Library is European in form but universal in its anguish, and baião has a relationship with the infinite that is far more bodily than analytic philosophy usually permits. The result came out more epic than austere, which wasn't the plan, but the chorus "each letter a path, each book a god" inside a progressive rock arrangement acquired a hymn-like quality that works in a strange way.

What caught me in the lyric was the bridge: "if every book already exists, every destiny was already traced / between chance and order, man stands terrified." That's the right tension. The Library isn't comforting — it's paralyzing, if you let it be. The final strophe — "you'll see that the author is yourself in the infinite library" — is the exit that Borges's story explicitly refuses. But perhaps it's the exit music needs. Not every resolution has to be philosophical to be honest.

Listening to the final mix, I realize that the weight of the distorted guitars in the chorus attempts to compensate for a lack of real tension in the initial baião harmony. The intention was friction, but the result often sounds like two different songs playing in separate rooms. If I were to remake the track, I would anchor the rhythmic transition more explicitly, using the baião's triangle pattern not as an ornament, but as the mathematical time signature of the library itself. The library demands precision; the music delivered grandeur. Still, the attempt to fuse these two worlds reveals something about how we try to organize chaos: with noise and meter.
