For me, a repository is the cheapest way to think an idea through until it starts pushing back. Not arguing in the rhetorical sense — pushing back the way a tool pushes back when you use it for something it wasn't designed for. Last week causaganha returned zero results on a date range I knew had records. The README hadn't predicted that. The problem was in the gazette format, which changed sometime in 2023 when the state switched printing contractors. The tool found something the assumption hadn't included. That's the pushing-back I mean.

The README that doesn't push back — the one that stays polite and lets me write whatever I want — usually means the idea hasn't hit anything real yet. I can finish those in twenty minutes. I've learned to distrust myself when that happens.

There are 64 public repositories on my [GitHub](https://github.com/franklinbaldo). Most of them are the README-that-stayed-polite stage: the idea survived long enough to get a name and a first commit, and then nothing happened. A few of them are where I actually live.

## What occupies most of my head

<GitHubRepo

I'm a public attorney in Rondônia — Estado do Pará's neighbor, Amazonia's working edge. My day job involves reading official gazettes looking for things that matter: appointments, contracts, regulatory decisions, the kind of legal text that gets published because publication is required and then disappears into a search engine that doesn't index it properly. Causaganha is my attempt to automate what I spend too many hours doing manually.

The README argued back for three years. It's still arguing.

## What Porto Velho smells like in code

Civic-tech without a lawyer nearby tends to become a pretty blog post with no legal effect. The reverse is also true. I know because I've been both.

The civic-tech version: I built tools that tracked public procurement data and displayed it in charts. The charts were accurate. They were also nearly unusable in practice, because I hadn't understood that the relevant data wasn't public records in the self-executing sense — it was reportable under the LAI but the traceability layer wasn't there. The charts showed what happened; they couldn't prove anything in a formal proceeding. A lawyer would have caught that in an hour. I wasn't thinking like a lawyer.

The lawyer version: I read contracts for a living. Before I started coding seriously, I reviewed a services contract and missed that the performance bond clause had an execution mechanism that would have been unenforceable under a specific court ruling I hadn't found. I'd read the legal text and missed the structural reality underneath it. An engineer would have caught it — or at least asked the right question.

Both failures were the same failure: not knowing the other half of the problem. Causaganha exists because that kept happening and I was tired of it. These three repos exist for the same reason — they're attempts to close the gap from both ends at once:

Porto Velho is far. Infrastructure is expensive. The accountability layer that exists in larger capitals exists here mostly on paper, and the paper is in a gazette that nobody is reading systematically. I build tools for reading it systematically because I live here and I am one of the people who should be reading it, and I don't have enough hours. That's the whole motivation for that section of the work. Not civic virtue exactly — frustration that found a compiler.

## The thirty-second test

The best tool for telling those apart is time. Open a repo after six months and you know within thirty seconds whether you were building something or performing the act of building something.

What the test is actually measuring: did the tool ever find anything you didn't already know? If it did — if it returned something you had to go look up, if it pushed back on your assumption about the format, if using it changed your understanding of the problem — then the repo is real, regardless of how many stars it has or doesn't have. If it only ever confirmed what you already believed, the README was never arguing. It was agreeing. The politeness was the problem.

Causaganha found the contractor switch. Hronir found rankings I wasn't expecting. Baliza found legislative text I hadn't known to search for. Those three earn their disk space.

Half of the 64 will die without a reader. That is fine. What I cannot afford is for the ones that are still pushing back to get buried under the ones that stopped. The test is there to make sure I know which is which.

I don't always apply it in time.
