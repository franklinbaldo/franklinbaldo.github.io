## Composer Notes

"Nonada" is the first word of Grande Sertão: Veredas — and Guimarães Rosa chose it to open the entire novel because it contains everything and says nothing. A trifle. Almost nothing. Riobaldo begins telling his story with a gesture of minimization that is also a gesture of invitation: sit here, this is no big deal, but listen. The track was born from that tension — I wanted to create a guided meditation that used the voice of the sertão as vehicle, not as folkloric ornament. The narrator I wrote speaks like someone who has already walked far and knows that silence carries more weight than explanation.

The choice of elements in the prompt was deliberate: a baritone with a northeastern accent, fingerpicked viola in D major, cicadas, a breeze through juazeiro leaves. I wanted the sertão after rain — the moment when the earth breathes and you understand, physically, what relief means. Suno delivered exactly the texture I asked for: the deep voice that doesn't point upward, the airy reverb of the viola dissolving into harmonics. Sometimes the tool knows what it wants to say better than I imagined.

The line that matters most to me is "Viver é muito perigoso, já contaram" — "Living is very dangerous, so they say." It is directly from Riobaldo, or almost: the original is "Viver é muito perigoso." I added "so they say" because the meditation narrator is gentler than the ex-jagunço, but the quotation is intentional. There is an honesty of the sertão that is not available in the language of contemporary wellness. Riobaldo didn't find calm because he was tranquil; he found it because he had crossed something unbearable. The track closes with "Nonada" — the same gesture that opens the novel, repeated here as a closing, as if rest and beginning were the same thing.
