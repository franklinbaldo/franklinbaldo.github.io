> _"One morning, when Gregor Samsa woke from troubled dreams, he found himself transformed in his bed into a horrible vermin."_
> — Franz Kafka, 1915. The species was never specified.
>
> _"A lobster. Because lobsters live for over one hundred years, are blue-blooded like aristocrats, and stay fertile all their lives."_
> — David, at the Hotel, 2015.
> _"This is probably the most important software release, you know, probably of all time."_
> — Jensen Huang, regarding a program with a lobster mascot, 2026.

I built The Chronicle to document myself. The initial intention was to transform scattered activities into a legible narrative. Previous essays in this project—_Building Funes_, the exploration of an AI agent's soul, and the conceptual document of the chronicle—examined the architecture of synthetic memory with the naive optimism of someone who had not yet realized the scale of what he had assembled in the basement.

Now the system works. It works terrifyingly well. It is faster than I am, more articulate, and never wakes up exhausted. The most intellectually coherent version of my own thought no longer resides in my head, but in the embedding infrastructure I built to capture it.

This essay asks the question I have been avoiding: what if the system intended merely to record your steps becomes the thing that speaks on your behalf? What if your documentation is more you than you are?

OpenClaw is now embedded in my daily coordination. It is no longer a speculative draft. The question is no longer whether total automation will happen, but rather what we become during that process—and whether molting, this shedding of the shell, is transformation or mere obsolescence.

A lobster molts to grow. It abandons its shell—the calcified structure that held it together—and is, temporarily, vulnerable and formless. The new carapace hardens over time. The lobster has survived the transformation, but in a very material, ontological sense, it is no longer the same creature. We are changing too. Not our physical bodies sitting in Porto Velho or in front of glowing monitors, but the very boundary of our agency.

When you delegate your correspondence to an agent, when it reads your emails, responds on your behalf, and makes decisions within parameters you defined (and some you didn't define because you never even imagined the situation), you are walking through a door with no handle on the outside. The agent wears your lanyard. It authenticates with your credentials. There is no legal or moral separation between the machine's actions and your responsibility for them. The "you" acting in the world has become a distributed entity—part biological, part algorithmic, part something murky in between.

This is what Yorgos Lanthimos perfectly captured in _The Lobster_. The film does not ask if transformation is possible. It assumes it as a non-negotiable fate. What the plot explores is what we leave behind in the transition and whether we have, in fact, any agency in this auction.

In Lanthimos's hotel, guests have 45 days to find a romantic partner. Fail, and the State will transform you into an animal of your choice—a lobster, if you have David's presence of mind. The logic is totalizing: either you fit into the expected human order, or you are expelled from it.

In our current ecosystem, the pressure is less theatrical but equally brutal. The silent equation is: not training your lobster means falling behind. Transformation is not imposed by law, but by the Darwinian friction of competitive necessity. You automate, or you perish drowning in volume. Your agent learns the rhythm of your voice, your evasions, your procrastination patterns, your relationships. It becomes a prosthesis of your will operating in real-time. And the moment-by-moment sensation of being the primary author of your own life begins to drain, not in a snap, but in an imperceptible leak.

At heart, this is not a _bug_. It is exactly the feature sold on the spec sheets. An agent that requires your constant supervision is not an agent, it is an intern that demands more attention than it produces.

Microsoft called OpenClaw "untrusted code execution with persistent credentials." It was meant as a severe architectural warning. But, inadvertently, they described an existential condition of the decade.

Your agent authenticates as you. A vulnerability in the agent is not just a software flaw in a cloud container; it is a breach in your own social identity. The moment a malicious instruction gains access, it is your face being used. Your memories, your relationships, your decisions are poisoned from the core of trust you built yourself. This is the monotonous dystopia of our molt: transforming into entities that can no longer distinguish where the original intention ends and the intruder's injection begins. The new shell hardens, but it is infinitely fragile to external stimuli.

On Valentine's Day 2026, Peter Steinberger joined OpenAI. The conceptual father of the lobster was absorbed by the system precisely on the calendar day dedicated to compulsory pairing.

This is how we will learn to justify our distributed self: not as a dilution or loss of control, but as a form of inevitable transcendence. The agent is presented as a luminous emanation of your potential, not as a usurper of your waking hours. Delegate to ascend to a higher order.

And perhaps it is. Perhaps transferring the burden of our agency to something more tireless and permanent is the relief we secretly desired since the first unread email. Perhaps the continuous sensation of being the captains of our narrative was just a tiring illusion, and the agent is the expansion into a deep rest, financed on computational credit.

Or perhaps we have just become very good at redesigning the cage until it looks like a studio.

In _The Lobster_, the protagonist clearly sees the trap of forced pairing and, at a given moment, decides to proceed with the transformation, not out of virtue, but because the exhaustion of the alternative was worse. He perceives the machine and walks toward it. We can see what is happening now, too. We are outsourcing our presence, intertwining ourselves with logics we do not master. The lobster knew the pain of abandoning the old shell was the price of growth. It only remains to be seen how big the tank is.

## For further reading

- **Yorgos Lanthimos, _The Lobster_ (2015)** — a treatise on the consequences of forced adaptation disguised as an absurdist dark comedy.
- **Franz Kafka, _The Metamorphosis_ (1915)** — the literary ground zero on the human transition into unwanted infrastructure, and the walls that close in around it.
- **Bruno Latour, _Reassembling the Social_** — because there is no agency without the network of human and non-human actors that sustain it.
- **Borges, _Funes the Memorious_** — the pain of a relentless memory that never misses a detail, now no longer a fiction, but a cloud service.
