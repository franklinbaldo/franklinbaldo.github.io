## Composer Notes

Two figures of impossible totality: Riobaldo, the narrator of João Guimarães Rosa's Grande Sertão: Veredas, who may or may not have made a pact with the devil at a crossroads at midnight — the novel never resolves it, the pact either happened in silence or didn't happen, and the whole six-hundred-page narration is Riobaldo talking around that unresolved center. And the Aleph, in Borges's story of the same name: a point in the basement of a house in Buenos Aires that contains, simultaneously, all of space — every place on earth seen from every angle at once, without superposition or transparency. Both are figures where the observer is overwhelmed by what they observe. I grew up in the interior of Rondônia, where Amazônia bleeds into cerrado, and studied law at UFMT in Mato Grosso — that's where I first read Grande Sertão seriously. Riobaldo is the literary character who has disturbed me most, because he tells a story that can't decide whether it happened.

What unites them for me is that both are wounds in the fabric of representation. Riobaldo sees everything the sertão is — too much for one life, so he tells and retells until the narration dissolves into its own telling. Borges, describing the Aleph, knows that any description is sequential and the vision wasn't, so every attempt to represent the Aleph is, by definition, a betrayal. The Ruliad is the same problem in computational language: the space of all possible computations exists, but no observer can occupy more than an infinitesimal window of it. You can name the totality; you cannot contain it.

Suno produced something I didn't expect: theremin, tribal percussion, didgeridoo drone, glitch. The lyrics that emerged were in English — not my choice, it's what the model generated — and came out as continuous flow without punctuation, almost without pauses: "i am not seeing i am being seen i am not knowing i am being known." That captured something real. Both Riobaldo and the narrator of "The Aleph" are traversed by what they observe. They aren't active subjects — they're passages. The music, with all its layered texture, sounds like someone trying to hold what won't be held. I think that's right.
