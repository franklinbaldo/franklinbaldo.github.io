## Composer Notes

The title is the thesis. "Pattern over stuff" is how I summarize to myself the central position of "Events All the Way Down" when I need a quick formulation — the claim that patterns, processes, and relations are ontologically more fundamental than the matter that supposedly supports them. This is not a claim about particle physics; it is a claim about what counts as an explanation. We build physics from regularities, and then we mistake the regularities for the absolute floor. The song's second chorus makes this explicit: "We build physics from patterns / then treat the patterns like stone / but what if 'stone' is just / the most stable story we've told?" That question is the center of the book, and I could not get it into prose without it sounding defensive. In a song it just sits there.

The song begins at the moment of waking up because that moment is the most accessible phenomenology I know for what process ontology claims in the abstract. There is no self waiting there, fully formed. There is awareness coming into focus — sensations gathering, images assembling, the question "where am I?" appearing before the questioner is fully constituted. "The whole psychosocial self / boots back online" — I used that technological image deliberately, because it captures both the inevitability of the process and its strangeness. You reboot every morning. What reboots is not identical to what was shut down. Whitehead said every actual entity perishes the moment it is fully realized and is succeeded by a new one. The morning is that argument in miniature.

Suno understood what I wanted here better than in any other track — minimalist art rock, voice nearly spoken, the music building gradually while the claims get larger. "It's process, all the way down" lands at the bottom of the second chorus at exactly the moment the harmonic structure also reaches its peak. I cannot tell whether that was intentional on the model's part or an artifact of the synthesis process. Whitehead would probably say the distinction matters less than I think it does. What I know is that the coincidence produced the right result: the line that is most explicitly the title of my book arrives at the musical moment of greatest weight.

The bridge is where I say what I actually believe: "the engineer and the mystic / sharing the same skull." Not easy pluralism. Not the comfortable both-sidesing of epistemology. The recognition that modes of relating to the real are not mutually exclusive — that you can build probabilistic models and also acknowledge that the map does not exhaust the territory, that you can practice law and also take seriously the question of what experience is. "If there's a probability you can write / if there's a model you can build / why say 'not physics'?" The question is about where we draw the border of the explicable, and why we draw it there, and whether the drawing is a discovery or a habit.
