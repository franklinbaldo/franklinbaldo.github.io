## Composer Notes

The lyrics are not exactly mine. I took actual phrases from Ben Goertzel — from
a conversation with Jim Rutt on [The Jim Rutt Show, EP 330 "Worldviews"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yBjXif8jT6U),
published January 22, 2026, two days before this song. I listened; I versified.

The starting point is the question Jim asks at the top: what does it mean that
"Ben Goertzel woke up" — and in what universe? Goertzel's answer is the whole
first verse. That amorphous awareness floating in undefined space, thoughts and
images gradually crystallizing, sensations assembling, until the concrete
thoughts like "where am I?" or "what time is it?" emerge: "there's some awareness
sort of ambiently floating in some ill-defined space, and then thoughts and the
images gradually crystallize [...] then the full apparatus of a psychosocial self
is back." The self is not waiting there. It crystallizes. I took that nearly
verbatim.

The pre-chorus presents two frameworks Goertzel explores side by side: the
standard neuroscientific reading (a brain shifting states in a body in a room)
and the Buddhist phenomenological reading (reality crystallizing out of cosmic
nothingness, self and world as illusion). And he says he doesn't need to choose
between them — both are valid ways of understanding the same experience. "I don't
feel the need to choose" is nearly his phrase.

The title is the thesis. "Pattern over stuff" is how I summarize the central
position of _Events All the Way Down_ — the claim that patterns, processes, and
relations are ontologically more fundamental than the matter that supposedly
supports them. Goertzel anchors this in Peirce: firstness as raw unanalyzed
experience, secondness as reaction or collision between things, thirdness as the
relational patterns that bind them. "First there is raw experience / then
reaction / then relationship — / and pattern lives in relationship" is a direct
mapping of those three Peircean categories onto the lyrics. What we call
objective physical reality may be simply the most successful pattern that minds
have been able to sustain.

Goertzel notes that scientific data is patterns and irregularities observed over
time that we've collectively agreed to trust. "The data we call 'reality' / is a
web of relations / a history of observations / condensed into law" — and you can
do engineering without assuming there's some absolute thing behind the equations.
"We build physics from patterns / then treat the patterns like stone / but what
if 'stone' is just / the most stable story we've told?" I could not put that into
prose without sounding defensive. In a song it just sits there.

Suno understood what I wanted here better than in any other track: minimalist
art rock, voice almost spoken, the music building gradually as the claims grow
larger. "It's process, all the way down" — that line at the end of the second
chorus is where the book's title appears explicitly in the lyrics, and the model
placed it exactly where it needed to be, at the point where the harmonic build
also peaks. I don't know if that was intention or coincidence of the synthesis
process. Whitehead would probably say the distinction doesn't matter as much as
we think.

The bridge carries the dimension Goertzel explores most personally in the
interview: psychedelic experiences and the dissolution of self, where people
report contact with something vastly more expansive than individual consciousness
— including DMT experiences as momentary union with something infinitely more
intelligent before dropping back. "The engineer and the mystic / sharing the same
skull" — Goertzel says this about himself. Not easy pluralism. The refusal to
draw artificial borders between physics and the unexplained phenomena that might
simply require a wider conception of what physics is. "If there's a probability
you can write / if there's a model you can build / why say 'not physics'?" Years
of trying to write that in prose. The song was faster.

The final chorus draws on Goertzel's intuition about the ground of being as
fundamentally joyful and compassionate, even as it generates the chaotic
complexity we experience — and on Jeffrey Martin's work on fundamental
well-being, that persistent state beyond ordinary happiness. "And when the noise
of the person gets quiet / what's left feels strangely kind." Not proven. Not a
theorem. Just what remains when the mind is still.
