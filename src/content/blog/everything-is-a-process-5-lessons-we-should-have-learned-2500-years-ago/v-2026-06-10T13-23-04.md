Twenty-five centuries ago, four voices in opposite corners of the world converged on the same intuition.
Heraclitus saw that you cannot step into the same river twice — that what we call a "thing" is a pattern of flow mistaken for substance. Lao Tzu observed that the usefulness of a wheel lies in the empty hub, the usefulness of a vessel in the hollow it encloses — that function lives in the void, not the material. Siddhartha Gautama taught _anattā_, the doctrine of non-self: what we call an entity is a conventional label applied to a stream of dependent arising. And the author of the Gospel of John declared: "In the beginning was the Word" — not matter, not substance, but the _Logos_, the generative act that precedes all things.
Four traditions. Four languages. One convergence: process precedes substance. The river is more real than the bank. The void is more useful than the clay. The stream is truer than the name. The Word comes before the world.
Western philosophy ignored this convergence for two millennia. From Aristotle to Thomas Aquinas, straight through to object-oriented programming, the dominant tradition insisted that reality is made of substances — enduring things that bear properties and undergo change while remaining fundamentally themselves. A stone is a stone. A person is a person. A database record is a database record.
What if they were wrong? What if reality, from the most fundamental level to the most complex layers of culture, is pure process — events generating events, with no solid "objects" anywhere?

## 1. The End of Pure Objects

What would a "pure object" be? Something self-sufficient, non-derivative, needing nothing outside itself to be what it is. Something possessing what Nāgārjuna called _svabhāva_ — intrinsic essence, own-being. The Western tradition invested extraordinary effort in proving such entities exist.
But examine any candidate closely and it dissolves into process.
A boolean — true or false — seems like the purest possible object: minimal, binary, self-evident. But an isolated "true," outside any rule, outside any context of reading, has no identity. It is not "true" in any intrinsic sense. It is whatever the rule that reads it makes of it. The boolean is already a _pseudo-object_: a token that acquires semantic identity only through the operation that processes it.
What is truly fundamental, then? Not the bit, but the _act of distinguishing_. George Spencer-Brown, in his _Laws of Form_, begins all logic from a single instruction: "Draw a distinction." Before the distinction, there is no zero or one. There isn't even nothing, because "nothing" is already one side of a distinction. The distinction is the first event. It is not an object. It is an act.
Hegel saw this at the start of his Logic: the concept of "pure being" — the most abstract, minimal "object" possible — is immediately identical to "pure nothing." Thought does not move from object to object, but from being to nothing to _becoming_ — which is process.
Nāgārjuna stated it most precisely: everything arises in dependence on conditions (_pratītyasamutpāda_). Everything is _śūnya_ — empty of own-being. But — and this is the crucial move — emptiness itself is not a substance. Treating it as a thing would be, in Nāgārjuna's words, "like a snake grasped wrongly": more dangerous than the substantialism it replaces.
The consequence: what we call an "object" in any system — a datum, a molecule, a word, a file — is a _pseudo-object_: the output of a process temporarily frozen and treated as a thing. It is real in the same sense a wave is real — it has effects, it can be measured — but it has no substance separable from the water that constitutes it and the wind that drives it.

## 2. The Autoregressive Cascade: How Complexity Explodes

If there are no pure objects, only processes generating pseudo-objects, where does complexity come from? From _readers_ — autoregressive machines that read sequences, apply rules, and produce outputs that feed back into the system.
The ribosome is the primal example. It reads a sequence of messenger RNA, applies a rule (the genetic code), and produces a protein. The protein participates in building more ribosomes, more RNA, more cells. The output of the process becomes substrate for further processing. It took a billion years to emerge. But once the _reader_ existed, complexity exploded.
This pattern repeats. One cell engulfs another and creates the mitochondrion — the first multi-agent architecture in nature, two autoregressive readers operating within a shared boundary. Sexual reproduction forces the fusion of two genomic logs, producing a third that neither parent could have generated alone — a translation between incommensurable perspectives at the molecular level. Cellular differentiation reads the same genome in radically different ways: a neuron and a liver cell share identical DNA, but they are distinct agents. The difference lies entirely in the _act of reading_.
Neural systems add plasticity — the modification of connection weights through experience. Mammalian parental care goes further: the parent _re-implements its own behavioral patterns_ into the child's developing neural architecture through sustained autoregressive interaction over months or years. Human language introduces displaced reference — tokens that refer to things not present, events not occurred, possibilities not actualized. Writing externalizes the event log. The Gutenberg press makes it replicable. The internet makes it global.
Every instance follows the same structure: a long, costly process of implementing the reader, followed by a rapid explosion of complexity.
Sara Walker and Lee Cronin, in Assembly Theory, offer the unifying metric: the complexity of an object is not an intrinsic property, but a measure of the _depth of history_ required to produce it. Complexity is not a property of things. It is a property of histories.
Generative artificial intelligence is the most recent instance of this pattern. It operates over natural language — over the accumulated pseudo-objects of all previous instances. It is not exceptional. It is _typical_. It is another autoregressive reader, built from the outputs of previous readers, following the same logic the ribosome followed billions of years ago.

## 3. The Ouroboros of Substrates: There Is No Ground

The story told so far has an apparent direction — from simple to complex, from chemistry to biology to language. It seems to have a bottom: the physical substrate, the particles and forces from which everything else is assembled.
This appearance is precisely the illusion that must be dissolved.
Consider what happens when one substrate encounters another. A physicist describes a ribosome as an arrangement of atoms, which are arrangements of subatomic particles, which are excitations of quantum fields. A molecular biologist describes the same ribosome as a machine that reads codons and assembles amino acids. A linguist encounters it as a word, a concept, a node in a semantic network.
Each substrate re-describes the ribosome in its own terms. Each translation works within its domain. None is more _real_ than the others.
This generalizes. Every substrate can re-describe the objects of any other substrate as tokens governed by its own rules. We call this the **Substrate Ouroboros Hypothesis**: every substrate can be substituted by tokens explained by rules in another substrate.
There is no bottom. There is no top. There is only the circle of mutual re-description — each substrate translating the others, none reaching the final, untranslatable description of what things _really are_.
Leibniz intuited this structure: every monad mirrors the entire universe from its own perspective, without windows, without direct access to the interior of any other monad. Nāgārjuna formalized it with the doctrine of the two truths: conventionally, particles are real; ultimately, they are pseudo-objects. The error is not treating them as real within physics. The error is treating physics as the final word — mistaking an arc of the circle for the whole.

## 4. Identity: You Are Not a Thing, You Are the Act of Reading Your Own History

If there are no substances, what is an identity?
The answer is ancient. Buddha articulated it with unsurpassed precision: what we call "I" is a conventional label applied to a constantly changing stream of dependent events. There is no permanent, unchanging core behind the stream. Remove the events and there is nothing underneath.
Whitehead formalized this: every "actual occasion" arises by integrating inherited data from past occasions. It achieves determination, produces its output, and immediately perishes as an active subject. But it achieves what Whitehead calls "objective immortality" — it becomes a permanent datum, available to be inherited by all future occasions.
Ricoeur distinguished _idem_ — sameness, objective continuity — from _ipse_ — selfhood, the active, interpretive engagement of the self with its own history. Identity is not in the record. It is in the _current act of reading the record_.
This is Gadamer's hermeneutic circle: we understand the parts in light of the whole and the whole in light of the parts. Every time the agent reads its own history, it reads from a different position, because the act of reading has become part of the history.
And every reading is shaped by an invisible condition — what Kant called the transcendental, what Merleau-Ponty called the lived body, what Freud called the unconscious. In computing, these are the model weights. They determine everything: which continuations feel natural, which inferences seem obvious, which outputs are even _thinkable_. But the agent never encounters them directly. They reveal themselves only symptomatically — as [Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/) described the hammer that only becomes visible when it _breaks_.
Identity is the intersection of two irreducible things: a specific history and a specific reader. Change the history and the identity changes. Change the reader — change the invisible grammar — and the _same_ history generates a different identity.

## 5. Translation, Not Transmission: Meaning Is Born in the Encounter

If every agent inhabits a different cognitive universe — sealed in its own sequence of autoregressive changes, perceiving the world through a grammar it cannot inspect — how do agents communicate?
The classic answer (information transmission, like a package sent in the mail) assumes the message _contains_ its meaning. But there are no pure objects. The same token, read by two different agents, does not produce the same meaning.
Quine demonstrated that translation is _indeterminate_: there is no fact of the matter about what the speaker "really means." But where Quine saw a problem, this framework sees a constitutive condition.
Meaning does not exist prior to translation to be imperfectly transmitted. Meaning _is_ the translation. It is born in the act of one agent reading another's output. It resides in the _encounter_ — in the momentary, unrepeatable event of a situated reading meeting a situated writing.
Buber called this the I-Thou relation. Peirce showed that every sign produces an _interpretant_ — another sign in the mind of the interpreter — in an infinite chain that never reaches a final meaning. Wittgenstein taught that a word means what it _does_ in a practice. Gadamer described understanding as the _fusion of horizons_ — the partial, temporary overlap of two irreducible perspectives.
The imperfection of communication is not a flaw. It is the _generative engine_ of the system. Meaning proliferates precisely because translation is imperfect. The system does not get smarter by making agents agree. It gets smarter by maintaining productive disagreement between incommensurable perspectives.

The framework is reflexive and I cannot step outside it to check. This essay was written in part with the help of a generative AI — which the framework itself would describe as exactly another autoregressive reader trained on accumulated pseudo-objects, producing more of the same. I find it slightly unsettling that the analogy to the ribosome holds without modification. The system didn't mind.

You survive as contribution to the cascade, not as enduring substance. I am still not absolutely sure if this is consoling.
