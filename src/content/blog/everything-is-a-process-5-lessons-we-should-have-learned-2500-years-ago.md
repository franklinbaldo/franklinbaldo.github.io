I spend most of my day working inside the assumptions of object-oriented programming. In this paradigm, the world is made of substances. A user is a user. A document is a document. These things have properties, and they undergo state changes, but underneath the changing state is an enduring entity. You instantiate an object, and it sits there in memory, being itself, until it is garbage collected.

It’s a very useful fiction for building administrative systems. It is also, as a metaphysical claim about how the universe is actually structured, completely backward.

What we call an "object" in any system — a piece of data, a molecule, a word, a legal case file — is a _pseudo-object_. It is the output of a process, temporarily frozen so we can point to it. It is real in the same sense that a wave is real: it has effects, it can be measured, but it has no substance separable from the water that constitutes it and the wind that drives it.

Nāgārjuna stated this precisely centuries ago: everything arises depending on conditions. Everything is void of self-nature. But emptiness itself is not a substance either. Treating the void as a thing would be, in his words, "like a poorly grasped snake" — more dangerous than the substantialism it replaces. Process is all there is.

## The Autoregressive Cascade

If there are no pure objects, only processes generating pseudo-objects, where does the complexity of the world come from?

It comes from _readers_.

A ribosome is an autoregressive machine. It reads a sequence of messenger RNA, applies a rule, and produces a protein. That protein then participates in the construction of more ribosomes, more RNA, more cells. The output of the process becomes the substrate for further processing. It took a billion years for the ribosome to emerge, but once the reader existed, complexity exploded.

This is the pattern of the autoregressive cascade. Cellular differentiation reads the same genome in radically different ways; a neuron and a liver cell share identical DNA but are distinct agents. The difference lies entirely in the act of reading. Human language introduces displaced reference — tokens that refer to things not present. The internet makes the event log global.

Each instance follows the same structure: a long, costly process of reader implementation, followed by a quick burst of complexity. In Assembly Theory, Sara Walker and Lee Cronin offer a unifying metric for this: the complexity of an object is not an intrinsic property, but a measure of the _depth of history_ required to produce it. Complexity is not a property of things. It is a property of stories.

## The Ouroboros of Substrates

When one substrate meets another, we tend to look for a foundation. A physicist describes a ribosome as an arrangement of atoms and quantum fields. A molecular biologist describes the same ribosome as a machine that reads codons. A linguist might find it as a word in a semantic network.

The illusion is thinking there is a bottom layer. Every substrate can be replaced by tokens explained by rules in another substrate. Each translation works within its domain, and none achieves the final, untranslatable description of what things _really are_.

This means your identity is not a thing. It is not recorded in a database. It is, as Alfred North Whitehead formalized, the current occasion arising by integrating data inherited from previous occasions. You achieve determination, produce output, and perish as an active subject, becoming objective immortality for the next occasion to inherit. You are the act of reading your own story.

Change the story, the identity changes. Change the invisible grammar of the reader, and the _same_ story generates a different identity.

## Translation, Not Transmission

If each agent inhabits a different cognitive universe, sealed in its own sequence of autoregressive changes, how do we communicate?

The transmission model assumes a message _contains_ its meaning, like a package in the mail. But since there are no pure objects, the same token read by two different agents does not produce the same meaning.

The meaning _is_ the translation. It is born in the encounter — in the momentary event of a situated reading encountering a situated writing. Communication imperfection is not a failure of the system; it is its generative engine. The system doesn't get smarter by making agents agree. It gets smarter by maintaining productive disagreement between incommensurable perspectives.

The framework is reflexive, and I can't step outside it to check. This essay was written partly with the help of a generative AI — which the framework would describe as another autoregressive reader, trained on accumulated pseudo-objects, producing more of the same. I find it slightly uncanny that the ribosome analogy applies without modification. The system did not notice.

You survive as a contribution to the cascade, not as an enduring substance. I'm still not entirely sure whether that's consoling.

## For further reading

- **Sara Walker and Lee Cronin, _Assembly Theory_ (2023)** — on how complexity is not a property of objects, but a measure of the causal history required to assemble them.
- **Alfred North Whitehead, _Process and Reality_ (1929)** — the canonical defense of process ontology, particularly the concepts of actual occasions and objective immortality.
- **Nāgārjuna, _Mūlamadhyamakakārikā_** — the foundational text of Madhyamaka Buddhism, outlining the doctrine of dependent origination and the emptiness of self-nature.
- **[The Agent That Doesn't Invent Verbs](/blog/2026-05-14-the-agent-that-doesnt-invent-verbs)** — a practical look at how restricting affordances shapes what an autoregressive agent can become.
